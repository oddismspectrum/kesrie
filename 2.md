## Pronouns and verbs
Now that we've seen how the language is pronounced, let's check out some words. Here are some pronouns:
- **mi** I, me
- **do** you (can be either one person or multiple people)
- **ti** this (as in "this thing right here")

And here are some verbs:
- **tcidu** 'read'; ... reads ...
- **xabju** 'live in, inhabit'; ... inhabits ...

With these five words, you can already make a few sentences. The typical word order in Lojban is just like in English, subject–verb–object.

```
mi tcidu
I  read
'I read.'
'I'm reading.'

mi tcidu ti
I  read  this
'I'm reading this.'

do  xabju   ti
you live-in this
'You live in this [place].'
'You live here.'
```

There is one thing that makes Lojban different from most other languages, though. In most natural languages, we express concepts like "book", "person", or "yellow" with nouns and adjectives, which work in a different way than verbs. In Lojban, *every content word* (a word that has a concrete meaning, as opposed to being just "grammar") *is a verb*. It might seem weird at first to think of "booking", "personing" and "yellowing" as being verbs in the same way as running, reading, playing, and sleeping, but this is the way Lojban does it. So, if after you said *mi tcidu ti* 'I'm reading this', someone asks you 'what is this?', you'd reply:

```
ti   cukta
this book
'This is a book.'
```

*cukta* and *pelxu* are verbs in the same way that *tcidu* or *xabju* are. Similarly, *stizu*, the word for chair, doesn't just mean 'chair', it means '... is a chair'. If for some reason you had been transformed into a chair by some evil wizard's curse, you could say:

> mi stizu
> 'I'm a chair!'

and this sentence would have the same structure as *mi tcidu*, because just like you can read (and that's a verb), in Lojban you can "chair".

### Verb places
Every verb has a set number of places. *stizu*, '... is a chair', only has one place: if something is a chair, or "chairs", it just chairs. It's not "chairing" anything. (Yes, someone could be sitting on the chair, and so on — but a chair is still a chair even if there's no one sitting on it. So a chair just chairs.) This means that, for example, saying:

> ×ti stizu mi
> 'This chairs me.'

would be nonsensical. But there are also verbs that have more than one place: *tcidu*, '... reads ...', has two places — reading involves a reader and a thing being read (a book, a sign, anything with text on it). So while you can't say ×*ti stizu mi*, you can say *mi tcidu ti* for 'I read this', like we've seen before. I could also say *mi tcidu do*, 'I'm reading you', if for some reason I encountered a sign that could understand Lojban. (Bear with me with the silly examples for a while — when we learn more words, they'll get more realistic.)

There are also verbs that have three places. A good example of this is *dunda*, the word for giving:

> **dunda** ... gives ... to ...; \[giver] gives \[gift] to \[receiver]

> [!info] Word definitions
> In this textbook, every time a verb is defined, we'll mark the places it has with three dots, like so: ‹...›. For each time you see this, it means the verb has one more place: one more blank that you can fill in with information. The order of these blanks matters: *mi dunda ti do* 'I give this to you' is very different from the odd *mi dunda do ti* 'I give you to this'.

This is quite rare, but some words have more than three places. *vecnu*, 'sell', is an example of a word with four:

> **vecnu** ... sells ... to ... for price ...

You would fill these just as you would *dunda*. Except we don't know any numbers yet, so we can't really say the price of things — but that's not a problem, because you don't have to fill every place of a verb. (We've seen this above with *mi tcidu* as well.) If I say "I sell you this", you know that I sold it to you for *some* price, even if you don't know what the price is. So we can say:

> mi vecnu ti
> 'I sell this.' (to someone, and for some price, but we don't say it.)

> do vecnu ti mi
> 'You sell this to me.' (here also for some price, but we don't say it.)
