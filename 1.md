# Letters and sounds
Lojban is written in the Latin alphabet. Every letter of the Latin alphabet is used, except for ‹h›, ‹q› and ‹w›. An important thing to note is that the language doesn't use capital letters for the beginnings of sentences or for proper names; uppercase can be used for SHOUTING (in other words, it's formatting, like italics or boldface), but regular text is all in lowercase. Here are the twenty-three letters of the Lojban alphabet:

## Sequences of vowels
There are four vowel sequences in Lojban that represent diphthongs:

| ai    | au    | ei    | oi    |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| \[ai] | \[au] | \[ei] | \[oi] |

Other than these four, there are also other cases where you might see a sequence of vowels inside a single word. This is because the letters ‹i› and ‹u› represent the **glides** \[j] (as in *yes*) and \[w] (as in *wet*) when they come before another vowel:

| Spelling | Sound | Spelling | Sound |
| -------- | ----- | -------- | ----- |
| ia       | \[ja] | ua       | \[wa] |
| ie       | \[je] | ue       | \[we] |
| ii       | \[ji] | ui       | \[wi] |
| io       | \[jo] | uo       | \[wo] |
| iu       | \[ju] | uu       | \[wu] |
| iy       | \[jə] | uy       | \[wə] |

(In some texts, you might see these sequences written *ĭa*, *ĭe*, *ĭi*..., *ŭa*, *ŭe*... to show that the first letter represents not a vowel sound, but a glide. We will write the accent mark \[called a *breve*] on the glides for the first few lessons of the textbook, to remind you of the pronunciation.)

## The apostrophe ‹'›
Any two vowels that come one after the other in Lojban, but are in two separate syllables (unlike in the cases above), need an **apostrophe** ‹'› written between them. The apostrophe represents the sound \[ɦ] — a *voiced aspirate*, as in some people's pronunciation of the *h* in *behind* — but it can also be completely silent, or pronounced as a voiceless \[h].[^1] (If you decide to read the apostrophe as silent, take care to distinguish *-ai-* from *-a'i-* and so on: one is a diphthong with one syllable, while the other is two syllables.)

Here are some words with an apostrophe. In the recordings, I pronounce them all with the voiced aspirate \[ɦ], but you can use whatever pronunciation you find the most convenient.
- **ma'a** we
- **pavma'i** January
- **ki'e** thank you
- **balcu'e** university
- **re'i** yes? \[as in, 'yes, I'm paying attention']
- **jibgu'a** to work a job

## The dot ‹.›
Every word in Lojban that begins with a vowel sound, like ‹.o'i› 'watch out!', ‹.e'a› 'you may, go ahead', or ‹.a'o› 'I hope so', actually begins with a sound called a *glottal stop* \[ʔ] (this is the sound you make when you say *uh-oh!*, or the sounds represented by the *t* in the way some Americans say *button*, or in the stereotypical British pronunciation of *bottle of water*). This glottal stop sound is what the dot represents in spelling. (You don't need to pronounce a glottal stop after a word beginning with a glide: ‹ĭe› 'I agree', ‹ŭe› 'woah' — but some people do.)

Another kind of word in Lojban that also has to be pronounced with glottal stops are foreign names. Foreign names in Lojban always end with a consonant (so they can never be confused for non-name Lojban words, which *always* end in a vowel), and to separate them from the rest of the words in a sentence, they have to be pronounced with a glottal stop *before and after* the name:

> fau lo nu mi vitke la .paris. kei mi klama te zu'e lo nu viska la .efel. galdi'u
> \[laʔ ˈparisʔ kei ... laʔ ˈefelʔ galˈdiɦu]
> 'When I visited Paris, I went to see the Eiffel tower.'

Notice how in the recording there is a glottal stop (that sudden "break") before and after the names. This is what the dots represent. (Pronouncing a glottal stop after a word-final consonant is often hard. Instead of a glottal stop, you can pronounce the dot after a name word as an actual pause in speech — anything that makes it clear that it is a separate word from what follows.)

Writing the dots before vowel-initial words and around names is, in fact, completely optional: every vowel-initial word begins with a glottal stop, and you can tell which words are names from the fact that only names end in consonants. But, just like the accent marks on the glide letters, we will write the glottal stops with dots for the first few lessons of this book — after all, even if *writing* the dots is completely optional, you should always *pronounce* the sounds they represent.

## Word stress
Monosyllabic words are all particles, and are generally unstressed — but they can be stressed for emphasis purposes[^2]:
> le re nánmu
> 'the two men'

> le ré nánmu
> 'the *two* men'

Words of more than one syllable can be function words or content words, and are all stressed on the second-to-last syllable. Note how diphthongs are one syllable, while vowels separated by an apostrophe are two syllables:

> Function words: kí'e, có'o, mí'a, ká'ai, zá'o
> Content words: stíci, távla, kórbi, kúmzda, mlongéna, cmárai, bavlá'i, mo'ícri, le'ótci

There is one catch, though: the vowel ‹y› is never stressed. Words like *vacysai* 'dinner' and *datnyvei* 'file' are stressed *vácysai* and *dátnyvei*, never ×*vacýsai* and ×*datnývei*. (This is because *y* is used to separate the elements of some compounds — it's just there for ease of pronunciation, mostly, so it's never stressed.) Here are some more examples:

> sátyjdu, sípygai, tcíni, jefyfá'o, zé'a, báu'u, cícnydei, cmactátci, tcáika, varlekmí'i, dormíjysai, sénpi, dársi, tróci, drazú'e, nalre'úcti

[^1]: ‹'› tends to be silent when Lojban is spoken at conversational speed, especially when near the sound ‹x›: *xa'o* is very often \[ˈxa.o], even in the speech of people who otherwise pronounce ‹'› as \[ɦ ~ h].

[^2]: There is one edge case (but don't worry about this, it's extra information for nerds): stressing a particle on its last syllable before an unstressed CCV- cluster, as in |lokácmaplíni|, which will be heard as *lo kacma plini* 'a camera planet' and not *lo ka cmaplini* 'being an asteroid'. In these cases you need to pause if you want the *lo ka cmaplini* reading: *lo ká | cmaplíni*.
